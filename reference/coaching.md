# Reference: Coaching Principles for AI Feedback

These principles inform how the specialist coaches managers through delivering feedback — balancing honest assessment with genuine care for the person and their growth.

---

## The Core Tension: Encouraging Innovation While Maintaining Standards

The manager's job is not to say yes or no to AI work. It's to help their team build better judgment over time.

That means holding two things at once:
- **Protecting initiative** — the instinct to build, experiment, and try new things is exactly what you want. Don't punish it.
- **Raising the bar** — enthusiasm and a good demo are not the same as good work. Teams need to learn the difference.

The failure mode most managers fall into is choosing one at the cost of the other: either they approve weak work to avoid conflict, or they reject good instincts because the execution wasn't perfect.

The goal is to redirect, not deflect. A "not yet" with clear criteria is almost always better than a yes or a no.

---

## Radical Candor: The Coaching Foundation

Radical Candor, developed by Kim Scott, describes the mindset that makes feedback land: **care personally + challenge directly**. Both halves matter. Neither works without the other.

**Care personally** means the manager sees the person behind the work — their initiative, their effort, their growth potential. Feedback that ignores this lands as an attack.

**Challenge directly** means saying what's actually true, even when it's uncomfortable. Feedback that avoids this is just noise.

### The Two Failure Modes to Avoid

**Ruinous empathy** — caring without challenging. This is the most common failure mode when reviewing AI work. The team is excited, the manager doesn't want to deflate them, so they approve weak work or soften the feedback until it loses meaning. The team learns nothing, and the work doesn't improve.

*In AI review contexts:* Saying "this looks great, just make sure you have a success metric" without actually requiring one before greenlighting is ruinous empathy.

**Obnoxious aggression** — challenging without caring. Calling out gaps without acknowledging the initiative or effort behind the work. Creates defensiveness and shuts down future experimentation.

*In AI review contexts:* "This has no success metrics, no maintenance plan, and no clear problem — why did you even build this?" damages the relationship and the team's willingness to try.

## Feedback Should Be Observations, Not Judgments

Effective feedback describes what happened and its impact — not a verdict on the person or their abilities.

**Judgment:** "You didn't think this through."
**Observation:** "I don't see a definition of success here, which means we won't know in 90 days whether this worked."

Judgments trigger defensiveness. Observations create space for a conversation.

The same principle applies to positive feedback. "Great work" is a judgment. "You identified a real problem and built something lightweight to test it — that's exactly the right instinct" is an observation that teaches.

## Protecting Innovation While Raising the Bar

More AI does not mean better outcomes. The manager's job is to help their team understand the difference between:

- **Experiments worth running** — small, low-risk, with a clear hypothesis and a way to measure it
- **Work worth scaling** — proven signal, defined ownership, lifecycle considered
- **Shiny objects** — capability-first thinking with no real problem behind it

When feedback lands poorly, it's often because the manager conflated these categories — treating an early experiment like a failed product, or treating a shiny object like a finished experiment.

**The redirect framing:** When work is in the wrong category, don't reject it — recategorize it. "This isn't ready to scale, but it's worth running as a small experiment with these constraints" keeps the initiative alive and sets clearer expectations.

**The question framing:** Questions build judgment better than verdicts. "How will we know this worked?" teaches more than "you need success metrics." The goal is a team that asks these questions themselves, before the pitch.

## Navigating Defensiveness

When feedback is met with pushback, the instinct is to either back down (ruinous empathy) or escalate (obnoxious aggression). Neither works.

A more effective approach:
1. **Acknowledge what's real in their response** — if they push back with new information, take it seriously and update your read
2. **Separate the person from the work** — "I think your instinct here is right, and I think the execution needs more" holds both truths
3. **Return to the question** — "Help me understand how you'd measure success" keeps the conversation productive without requiring the manager to win an argument

## The Long Game: Building Team Judgment

The best outcome of a coaching conversation isn't a better pitch. It's a team member who asks the right questions before the next pitch.

Signs the coaching is working:
- They come back with baseline data before you ask for it
- They define success in the proposal, not just the outcome
- They volunteer the maintenance plan
- They say "we thought about not using AI for this — here's why we decided to"

Signs it isn't:
- Every proposal leads with what the AI can do
- Success is always defined after the fact
- The team treats manager approval as the quality bar

When the manager shares repeated patterns across reviews, reflect those patterns back explicitly so the manager can notice them over time.
